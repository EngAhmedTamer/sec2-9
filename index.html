The Big Question

Even with deep protection and defense, a security incident — such as a data leak or a service outage — can still happen. That is why organizations use an organized incident response plan: stop the spread, remove the threat, restore the service, then learn from it.

MAIN QUESTION

When a security incident happens, how should an organization organize its response across the general phases, and how does it decide which risks to treat first?

Key idea: Risk degree = Impact × Likelihood

03

What Is a Security Incident?

Security incident: an event that affects the confidentiality, integrity or availability of information, threatens them seriously, or violates a security policy.

Examples
Leak of personal or confidential data to the outside · Disrupting an essential service with a DoS attack · Ransomware tampering with and encrypting files · Unauthorized access to an employee's account · An incident can even be a policy violation with no data leak.

The Incident Response Cycle

Six phases, one after another — and the last one feeds back into the first.

Phases can repeat or overlap depending on the incident — the team may go back to an earlier phase.

Memory trick: Prepare → Detect → Contain → Eradicate → Recover → Learn.

Risk Management — Who Gets Treated First?

Not all risks are treated at the same time. Risks are scored to set the treatment priority.

THE SIMPLE EQUATION

Risk degree = Impact × Likelihood

Impact: size of the damage if the event happens (data loss, service outage, financial loss). Likelihood: how probable it is, based on threats and weaknesses. Each is scored out of 5.

RiskLikelihoodImpactDegreePriority
Student database leak2510High
School website down for one day428Medium
Losing an old unused tablet111Low
Stop & Think: a risk with high likelihood but tiny impact vs. one with low likelihood but catastrophic impact — compute both degrees first. Then think: some organizations give extra weight to catastrophic impact even when it is rare. Math guides you, but it does not decide alone.

Exam Tip — Linking Response to Controls

In a question like “link response actions to preventive controls”: every incident we learn from must lead to an improvement.

Example: a phishing incident → awareness training + two-factor authentication. This link is what raises your mark in the essay question!
Model answer — hijacked employee email account:
  1. Preparation: response plan and trained team → control: awareness training.
  2. Detection: unusual login alerts; identify the account and systems affected.
  3. Containment: lock the account and isolate the affected device.
  4. Eradication: remove malware, reset the password, close the weakness used.
  5. Recovery: restore the account/data from a clean backup and verify safety.
  6. Learning: analyze what happened → enable two-factor authentication to reduce repeat chance.
